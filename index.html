Crockpot.

A British English automated writing checker. Spelling comes from a 114,500-word dictionary and edit-distance maths; the grammar, punctuation and clarity notes come from a hand-written rule engine that reads each sentence word class by word class.

Spelling. Checks run against a (roughly) 114,500-word British English dictionary built from the SCOWL British list, so “colour”, “organise” and “travelled” are the dictionary forms. Candidate corrections are generated by known-edit search (deletions, insertions, substitutions, transpositions, including the apostrophe, so “dont” reaches “don’t”), then ranked by a confusion-weighted edit distance that treats vowel swaps, sound-alike letters, doubled letters and neighbouring keys as cheaper than random changes, with a phonetic key as a fallback for errors like “nite” → night. American spellings are simply absent from the dictionary, so the same engine that catches “recieve” catches “color”.

Grammar

Sentences are split, tokenised and tagged with a lightweight word-class layer: closed classes (pronouns, determiners, modals, auxiliaries, prepositions) are listed exhaustively, open classes are recognised through frequency lists plus morphology, these includes; irregular verb tables, plural and third-person endings, participle forms. On top of that sit rules for subject–verb agreement, verb form after modals and auxiliaries (“could of”, “have went”, “he go”), determiner–noun number, a/an by sound rather than spelling, fewer/less, double negatives and doubled comparatives, and the real-word confusions a spellchecker structurally cannot see, (/there/they’re, your/you’re, its/it’s, then/than, to/too, lose/loose, affect/effect), each decided from the words on either side rather than flagged on sight. Rules are written to stay silent when the context is ambiguous: British collective nouns keep their plural verbs (“the team are winning”), and a word already marked as misspelled is never given grammar advice on top.

Punctuation and clarity

Spacing, missing apostrophes in real words (“cant”, “wont”, “hell go”), capitalisation of sentences, “I” and proper nouns, comma splices, unbalanced brackets and quotes; then a clarity pass for passive voice, wordy phrases, redundant pairs, hedging intensifiers and over-long sentences. The score is a weighted issue density, and reading ease is the Flesch formula over the syllable counts. Everything is computed in the browser from the rules above.
